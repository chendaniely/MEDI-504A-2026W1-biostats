Interpreting a logistic regression

Packages

library(tidyverse)
library(tidymodels)
library(medicaldata)

A sore throat after surgery

licorice_gargle (from medicaldata) is a trial of 235 adults having thoracic surgery with a large breathing tube. Before the tube went in, they were randomized to gargle licorice or sugar water. Our outcome is any sore throat 30 minutes after surgery.

Recode the variables

sore <- licorice_gargle |>
  mutate(
    sore_throat = factor(pacu30min_throatPain > 0,
                         levels = c(FALSE, TRUE),
                         labels = c("no", "yes")),
    treatment = factor(treat, levels = c(0, 1),
                       labels = c("sugar", "licorice")),
    smoking = factor(preOp_smoking, levels = c(3, 2, 1),
                     labels = c("never", "past",
                                "current"))
  ) |>
  select(sore_throat, treatment,
         bmi = preOp_calcBMI, smoking) |>
  drop_na()

Which level are we modelling?

  • logistic_reg() models the odds of the outcome’s second level
  • sore_throat has the levels no, then yes, so every result is about the odds of a sore throat
  • The predictors are a mix of both kinds:
    • treatment: 2 levels, so 1 dummy variable (reference sugar)
    • bmi: continuous, in kg/m²
    • smoking: 3 levels, so 2 dummy variables (reference never)
count(sore, sore_throat)
  sore_throat   n
1          no 169
2         yes  64

Fit the model

sore_fit <- logistic_reg() |>
  fit(sore_throat ~ treatment + bmi + smoking,
      data = sore)

  • exponentiate = TRUE turns each estimate and its interval into an odds ratio (std.error, statistic, and p.value stay on the log-odds scale)
  • An odds ratio of 1 means no difference, below 1 means lower odds, and above 1 means higher odds

Interpreting a continuous variable

For every 1 [unit] increase in [variable], we expect the odds of [outcome] to be multiplied by [odds ratio], all else remaining the same.

For bmi:

For every 1 kg/m² increase in BMI, we expect the odds of a sore throat to be multiplied by 0.95, all else remaining the same (95% CI: 0.88 to 1.02).

  • Multiplied by 0.95 is the same as 5% lower odds
  • The interval includes 1, so the data don’t show a clear effect of BMI

Interpreting a categorical variable

We expect the odds of [outcome] for [level] to be [odds ratio] times the odds for [reference level], all else remaining the same.

For treatmentlicorice:

We expect the odds of a sore throat for patients who gargled licorice to be 0.38 times the odds for patients who gargled sugar water, all else remaining the same (95% CI: 0.20 to 0.71).

  • That’s 62% lower odds, and the interval doesn’t include 1

A categorical variable with more levels

Each dummy variable compares one level with the reference, never. For smokingpast:

We expect the odds of a sore throat for past smokers to be 4.38 times the odds for people who never smoked, all else remaining the same (95% CI: 1.94 to 10.48).

  • smokingcurrent compares current smokers with people who never smoked, not with past smokers
  • Its interval (0.86 to 4.33) includes 1, so that comparison isn’t clear

Odds, not probability

  • An odds ratio compares odds, not probabilities
  • Say “0.38 times the odds”, not “0.38 times as likely” or “62% less likely”
  • The two are close only when the outcome is rare, and here 27% of patients had a sore throat

The same model in base R

Base R’s glm() fits the same model with less code:

glm(sore_throat ~ treatment + bmi + smoking,
    family = binomial, data = sore) |>
  tidy(exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)

  • family = binomial is what makes glm() a logistic regression
  • The same odds ratios and intervals as logistic_reg()
  • We use tidymodels because every kind of model goes through the same steps
